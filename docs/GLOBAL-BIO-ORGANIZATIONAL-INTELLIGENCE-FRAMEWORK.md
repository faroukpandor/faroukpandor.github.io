# Global Bio-Organizational Intelligence Framework (GBOIF)

**Status:** FOUNDATIONAL SCOPE — research architecture approved in conversation; implementation and scientific validation pending  
**Programme owner:** Farouk Pandor (user-provided)  
**Geographic scope:** Global  
**Regional application priority:** Botswana and SADC, without limiting research coverage  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-07

## 1. Purpose

Establish an evidence-led, globally scoped research and knowledge framework that investigates biological diversity and biological systems, then responsibly translates validated knowledge into scientific, agricultural, veterinary, environmental, technological and enterprise applications.

This is a research and knowledge architecture, not a claim that all biodiversity is already catalogued or that every biological discovery has a viable commercial use.

## 2. Scope: all biodiversity and related biological entities

Coverage is global and includes known, under-described and potentially undescribed biological diversity across terrestrial, freshwater, marine, subterranean, aerial, soil-associated, host-associated and extreme environments.

### 2.1 Cellular life

- Bacteria
- Archaea
- Eukaryotes, including animals, plants, fungi, and diverse protists and microbial eukaryotes
- Unicellular and multicellular organisms
- Symbiotic, commensal, parasitic, pathogenic and free-living organisms

### 2.2 Acellular and subcellular biological entities

These must be represented explicitly but must not be incorrectly classified as cellular organisms or conventional species:

- Viruses, including bacteriophages, archaeal viruses, giant viruses and satellite viruses
- Viroids and satellite nucleic acids
- Prions and other protein-based infectious agents
- Plasmids and other mobile genetic elements
- Transposons, endogenous viral elements and other genomic elements where relevant

### 2.3 Communities, biological systems and environments

- Microbiomes and microbiota, including soil, plant, animal, human, aquatic, marine, built and extreme-environment communities
- Biofilms, microbial consortia and host–microbe relationships
- Food webs, ecological networks, symbioses and ecosystem processes
- Genes, genomes, metagenomes, populations, strains, breeds and cultivated varieties

### 2.4 Human-associated and engineered biological systems

Include domesticated livestock, companion animals, crops, horticultural varieties, selectively bred lines, industrial microbial strains, genetically modified organisms, synthetic biology constructs and laboratory model organisms. Record origin and intervention status separately: wild/naturally occurring, domesticated, selectively bred, cultured/isolated, genetically modified, engineered or synthetic. Do not conflate these categories.

## 3. Scientific domains

1. Taxonomy, systematics and nomenclature
2. Microbiology, bacteriology, archaeal biology and virology
3. Mycology and protistology
4. Botany, zoology and marine/freshwater biology
5. Molecular biology, genetics, genomics and evolutionary biology
6. Microbiomes, symbiosis and host–microbe interactions
7. Ecology, ecosystem science and biogeography
8. Extremophile biology and astrobiology-relevant life sciences
9. Conservation biology, restoration and invasive-species research
10. Biotechnology, bioengineering and synthetic biology
11. Agricultural, veterinary, food and One Health applications

## 4. Evidence and data governance

Every claim must retain source provenance and an explicit evidence status:

- **VERIFIED:** directly supported by a traceable primary source, authoritative dataset, specimen record or validated analysis.
- **DOCUMENTED:** supported by a credible secondary source or established database; primary evidence may still need review.
- **REPORTED:** stated by a source but not independently corroborated.
- **INFERRED:** a reasoned interpretation, clearly separated from direct observation.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** a testable proposition requiring investigation.
- **UNKNOWN:** evidence is absent, conflicting, inaccessible or insufficient.

Record scientific name as supplied, accepted name and authority where verified, taxonomic backbone and version/date, synonyms, identifier, rank, classification confidence, source URL/DOI/accession, observation or specimen provenance, collection date, geospatial precision, licence, rights/consent restrictions and last-reviewed date.

Do not equate missing records with absence, rarity, endemism, extinction risk or novelty. Taxonomy changes; retain historical names and identifiers and document reconciliation rather than silently overwriting them.

## 5. Research record model

Each record should distinguish, where applicable:

1. Entity identity and classification
2. Biological type (cellular organism, acellular agent, genetic element, community, population, ecosystem or engineered system)
3. Taxonomic and nomenclatural status
4. Geographic and environmental context
5. Habitat, host and ecological relationships
6. Morphology, physiology, metabolism and life history
7. Genome, sequence, strain, specimen or voucher references
8. Functional roles and known interactions
9. Conservation, health, agricultural or biosecurity relevance
10. Potential applications and limitations
11. Evidence matrix, references, uncertainty and research gaps
12. Access, collection, data-use and benefit-sharing conditions

Use recognised identifiers and standards where appropriate (for example, GBIF, Catalogue of Life, NCBI Taxonomy, WoRMS, MycoBank, Index Fungorum, AlgaeBase, ICTV and relevant genomic/specimen repositories). These are complementary sources with different scopes, coverage and update cycles; none is a complete single source of truth.

## 6. Translation into systems intelligence and innovation

For each sufficiently supported biological finding, separately assess:

- Biological mechanism and ecological function
- Systems principle (for example cooperation, resilience, adaptation, resource cycling, distributed sensing or symbiosis)
- Potential agricultural, veterinary, environmental, industrial or digital application
- Evidence of technical feasibility and maturity
- Safety, regulatory, ecological and ethical constraints
- Intellectual-property, access and benefit-sharing considerations
- Validation pathway, performance indicators and unresolved questions

Classical literature and cultural knowledge may contribute historical observations or hypotheses, but must be labelled by provenance and assessed independently against modern biological evidence. Metaphorical enterprise lessons must never be presented as scientific findings.

## 7. Safety, ethics and responsible use

- Apply applicable biosafety, biosecurity, animal welfare, human-subject, environmental and research ethics requirements.
- Do not publish actionable pathogen enhancement, harmful-agent propagation, evasion or misuse instructions.
- Protect sensitive species localities, caves, breeding sites, protected habitats, genetic resources and community-held knowledge where disclosure could cause harm.
- Respect prior informed consent, access and benefit-sharing, Indigenous Peoples and local communities' rights, applicable national laws and the Nagoya Protocol where applicable.
- Do not imply permission to collect, sequence, export, commercialise or patent biological resources merely because data are publicly visible.
- Distinguish conservation status, pathogenicity, hazard, risk and commercial value; these are not interchangeable.

## 8. Portfolio routing and boundaries

- **Global programme master and governance:** this document in the personal portfolio control repository until a dedicated canonical programme repository is approved.
- **Existing organism dossiers:** retain organism-specific research in the relevant repositories. The current Botswana dossiers are regional case studies and must not be silently reclassified as global coverage.
- **AgriSage360 (AGRISAGE):** agricultural and livestock domain workflows, microbial/soil/plant/animal application requirements and farmer-facing decision support. It is not the global taxonomic master.
- **NEXUS-ONEHEALTH:** potential One Health integration across human, animal and environmental health. Its canonical role, data provenance and implementation maturity require review before integration.
- **MOKORO Enterprise OS:** portfolio governance and cross-project decisions only; do not store scientific source datasets or duplicate research dossiers there.
- **Client, third-party and upstream repositories:** no changes or asset reuse without the relevant authority and licence.

No new repository, public deployment, paid service, external partnership or collection programme is implied by this scope record. First perform overlap, ownership, licence, data-source and feasibility checks.

## 9. Initial staged roadmap

1. Inventory existing repositories, research assets, datasets and domain overlaps.
2. Define the entity model, taxonomic crosswalks, evidence schema and source register.
3. Build a source-quality and licensing matrix for global biodiversity repositories.
4. Pilot the model on a small, taxonomically diverse sample spanning microbes, viruses, fungi, plants, animals and ecosystems.
5. Audit scientific correctness, provenance, interoperability, privacy and sensitive-location controls.
6. Only after validation, decide whether to create a dedicated canonical repository and offline-capable research interface.
7. Develop application modules only where evidence and user demand justify them.

## 10. Immediate next action

Complete a read-only portfolio and data-source inventory. Do not create a duplicate global atlas, copy third-party datasets wholesale or expand every existing organism repository. Record the canonical destination and one next action for each relevant asset.

## 11. Current decisions

- Global biodiversity is the scope; Botswana and SADC are priority application regions, not geographic limits.
- Microbes are a major research priority.
- Viruses, prions, viroids and related entities are explicitly included, with scientifically correct non-cellular classifications.
- Domesticated, selectively bred, engineered and synthetic biological systems are included as separately labelled categories.
- The framework combines scientific discovery, ecological understanding and responsible application; commercialisation is conditional on evidence, safety, rights and demand.
