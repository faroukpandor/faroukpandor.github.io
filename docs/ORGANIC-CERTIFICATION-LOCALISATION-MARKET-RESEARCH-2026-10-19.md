# Market and Industry Research: Localising Organic Assurance and Certification in Botswana

**Research date:** 19 October 2026  
**Status:** Desk research and strategic hypothesis; not legal advice, not a market survey, and not proof of demand, certification recognition, or unit economics.

## Executive recommendation

**Do not begin by trying to create a new accredited certification body.** The strongest initial hypothesis is a locally operated **organic certification access and readiness service**, attached to AGRISAGE / AgriSage360 only after ownership and capacity are confirmed. It would help farmers form groups, maintain compliant records, prepare for inspection, coordinate local inspection visits, and work with an existing competent certification body. In parallel, assess a local participatory assurance pathway for suitable domestic short supply chains.

This approach can localise practical delivery, knowledge and farmer support without pretending that a local label automatically gives access to export markets. Keep certification decisions independent from paid advisory services. If the business later seeks to become a recognised certification body, treat that as a separate, expensive, multi-year institutional project.

## 1. What is verified

### 1.1 Global industry context

FiBL and IFOAM – Organics International reported in the 2026 edition of *The World of Organic Agriculture* that global retail sales of organic food and drink reached about **€145 billion in 2024**, with close to **99 million hectares** under organic management. Organic production is reported in more than 180 countries. These are global sector indicators, not an estimate of Botswana's organic market.

### 1.2 Botswana has an organic export programme in progress

Botswana Daily News reported on 21 April 2026, based on a parliamentary response, that:
- 160 farmers had been contracted under an organic turmeric export initiative for the 2025/26 and 2026/27 seasons.
- 24 farmers had planted during the 2025/26 season, across 22.3 hectares and eight districts.
- High start-up costs and difficulty securing finance were reported as barriers.
- Seeds were reported at approximately BWP 61,000 per hectare; full input costs, including certification, traceability and production, could reach BWP 300,000 per hectare per season.
- Projected production and revenue were reported, but these should be treated as forecasts rather than realised outcomes unless verified against harvest, sales and export records.
- The initiative was described as linked to a German company and a local agricultural firm, with plans to consider other high-value crops.

Source: https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/91078

**Interpretation:** This is a concrete sector lead and evidence that some export-oriented operators are already trying to organise organic production. It also signals that certification is only one component of the cost and risk problem. The service must not assume that lowering certification fees alone resolves finance, agronomy, yield, water, logistics, buyer-contract or working-capital constraints.

### 1.3 Botswana's quality infrastructure

The Botswana Bureau of Standards (BOBS) lists agricultural standards, testing, training and certification services. Its published certification page describes product certification and management-system certification, currently including ISO 9001. The pages reviewed do **not** establish that BOBS currently operates a dedicated organic certification scheme. This is a question to put directly to BOBS, not a claim that no relevant standard or arrangement exists.

Sources:
- BOBS agriculture services: https://bobstandards.bw/services/agriculture/
- BOBS certification: https://bobstandards.bw/certification/

FAO also reported in November 2025 that Botswana had developed and approved three national Codes of Practice related to priority value chains, alongside Good Agricultural Practices and quality-management capacity building. GAP and organic certification are not interchangeable, but this is relevant local standards infrastructure on which to build readiness services.

### 1.4 Existing certification providers and market structure

The industry already has several layers:
- **Independent third-party certifiers** inspect and make certification decisions against defined standards.
- **Local inspectors and technical specialists** can potentially reduce travel, scheduling and language costs when operating under the proper controls of a recognised certification body.
- **Farmer groups and internal control systems (ICS)** can organise smallholder documentation and internal monitoring for group certification where the target standard and certifier permit it.
- **Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS)** use active participation by producers and other stakeholders, commonly for local, short supply chains. They are not automatically equivalent to export-oriented third-party certification.
- **Readiness and recordkeeping services** help farmers meet requirements but must not represent themselves as certifiers unless appropriately authorised.

Examples:
- CERES South Africa says it certifies against EU Organic, USDA NOP, JAS and other standards, using local inspectors in South Africa: https://ceres-cert.co.za/
- The South African Organic Sector Organisation (SAOSO) explains the distinction between self-claims, PGS for short supply chains, and third-party certification for long supply chains and export: https://www.saoso.org/organic-certification/
- PGS South Africa describes group formation, agreed production principles, farm visits, status categories and annual member records: https://www.pgssa.org.za/organic-certification/
- IFOAM – Organics International supports PGS as an alternative or complement to third-party certification and explicitly distinguishes PGS from an internal control system used in group certification: https://www.ifoam.bio/our-work/how/standards-certification/participatory-guarantee-systems

**Important competitor/partner note:** Public information surfaced about regional certification providers and a Botswana organic turmeric initiative. A local business directory also carries a claim by Farmer's Pride International concerning an Ecocert award for moringa production in November 2025; this is a self-reported lead, not independent verification of the certificate's current validity, scope, products or target markets. Verify any such claims directly against the certifier and the certificate register before treating them as market facts.

### 1.5 Export recognition is market-specific

For EU-bound products, the relevant control body must be recognised for the country of origin and product category under the applicable EU organic import regime. The EU publishes and amends lists of recognised control bodies and countries. A body may be authorised for certain product categories and countries but not others; the current entry and scope must be checked before any commercial commitment.

The EU's 2025 amendment to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/1378 lists Kiwa BCS Öko-Garantie for Botswana in categories A, B, D, E and G. That is a useful lead for partner mapping, **not sufficient on its own to establish its exact status and scope on 19 October 2026**: check the latest consolidated EU list and contact the body directly.

Sources:
- EU regulation and current amendments: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02021R1378
- EU organic controls: https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/organic-farming/controls_en
- EU Implementing Regulation 2026/1398: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32026R1398
- USDA certifier locator and international rules: https://www.ams.usda.gov/services/organic-certification/certifying-agents and https://www.ams.usda.gov/services/organic-certification/international-trade

Do not infer that a local PGS label is sufficient for EU or US export. Confirm applicable law, the buyer's requirements, the exact standard, the certifier's authorisation, product category and the whole supply chain's status.

## 2. The likely customer problem

The opportunity should be tested around the following possible pain points rather than assuming a single “certification cost” problem:

1. **Distance and scheduling:** international or regional inspectors may require travel and advance scheduling.
2. **Readiness costs:** farms may lack records, input lists, plot histories, separation procedures, traceability and documented production plans.
3. **Group coordination:** smallholders may be individually too costly to inspect and administer, while group models require a functioning and credible internal control system.
4. **Finance and cash flow:** farmers may face large input costs before revenue; certification is only one part of the investment.
5. **Market certainty:** a certificate does not guarantee a buyer, price premium, accepted residue profile, export permit or profitable yield.
6. **Confusing claims:** producers and buyers may not understand the difference between organic practices, “in conversion,” local participatory assurance and export-recognised certification.
7. **Digital records:** paper-heavy or disconnected records may increase audit preparation costs, but technology only helps if the process is designed around the relevant standard and used consistently.

These are hypotheses to validate with farmers, certifiers, buyers and regulators. Do not market cost savings before measuring the current baseline and the new model.

## 3. Potential customer segments

| Segment | Need / willingness-to-pay hypothesis | Best initial offer |
|---|---|---|
| Export-oriented contracted farmers | High need for buyer-specific compliance, traceability and reliable certification; cost sensitivity may be severe | Group readiness, records, inspection coordination and cost transparency |
| Smallholders and producer groups selling locally | May need affordable trust and shared standards, but buyer demand and legal label rules must be confirmed | Participatory assurance feasibility pilot, farmer training and simple records |
| Aggregators, processors and exporters | Need traceability, consistent supply, group records and assurance continuity | Supplier onboarding, ICS support, chain-of-custody and audit preparation |
| Retailers, restaurants and institutional buyers | Need credible claims, dependable volumes and supplier verification | Supplier-verification directory and claim-status lookup; no unverified “organic” listings |
| Certification bodies | May value local logistics, competent inspectors, better-prepared clients and lower avoidable administration | Contracted local support or inspection services under a clear quality agreement |
| Public agencies, NGOs and development programmes | May fund producer capacity, standards adoption or inclusion, but procurement and funding cycles can be long | Transparent farmer-readiness programme with measurable outcomes |

## 4. Business model options

### Option A — Readiness and compliance service (recommended first)

Offer farm/group gap assessments, production-plan and input records, plot and harvest registers, traceability templates, staff training and pre-inspection checks. Charge a clear fee per farm or group, plus optional seasonal support. The service does not issue organic certificates or guarantee acceptance.

**Advantages:** lowest institutional barrier, can start small, builds local competence, directly addresses documentation friction.
**Risks:** farmers may have limited ability to pay; advisory work may create conflicts if the same entity also makes certification decisions; needs qualified agronomic and standards expertise.

### Option B — Local coordination and group-certification support

Work with a recognised certifier and producer organisation to organise group records, internal monitoring and locally based inspection logistics, where the certifier and target standard allow this.

**Advantages:** better chance of lowering duplicated travel/admin costs; connected to actual export requirements.
**Risks:** the certifier retains independent decisions; group systems need real monitoring, sanctions, corrective action and traceability; savings are not automatic.

### Option C — Participatory assurance for local markets

Support producers, consumers, local buyers and independent stakeholders to design a transparent PGS-type model, using clear criteria, farm visits, public status, complaints and corrective actions.

**Advantages:** potentially accessible for short supply chains; develops local knowledge and buyer trust.
**Risks:** buyer acceptance and applicable Botswana rules must be established; governance can be captured by members; should not be marketed as export-recognised third-party certification without proof.

### Option D — Establish a new accredited certification body

Build a legally and operationally independent certification organisation with qualified staff, procedures, impartiality controls, records, accreditation and target-market recognition.

**Advantages:** greater long-term local control if sufficient scale and institutional capability exist.
**Risks:** high cost and complexity, accreditation/recognition not guaranteed, ongoing audits and liability, potential conflicts if combined with consulting or product sales.

**Recommendation:** begin with Option A, test Option B with an established certifier, and assess Option C only for an evidenced local-market use case. Do not pursue Option D until producer volumes, recurring revenue, market recognition needs and the cost of accreditation justify it.

## 5. Standards, integrity and governance

Minimum controls should include:
- Public standard and transparent eligibility criteria.
- Clear distinction between advisory support and independent certification decisions.
- Conflict-of-interest declarations and controls over related-party decisions.
- Farm identity, plot history, permitted inputs, harvest and storage records, product segregation and traceability.
- Risk-based inspection and verification; documented evidence and corrective actions.
- Complaints, appeals, sanctions, suspension and withdrawal procedures.
- Protection of farmer and buyer data, access control, consent and retention rules.
- A public, verifiable status register that states the scheme, standard, product scope, validity dates and limitations.
- Independent periodic review and an anti-fraud process.
- No claims of accreditation, certification, equivalence, guaranteed premium, or market access without evidence.

Use international standards and buyer requirements as appropriate, but verify the applicable edition and legal route with qualified experts. Avoid suggesting that an organic scheme proves every environmental or health claim.

## 6. Market sizing: what can and cannot be calculated now

A reliable Botswana market size cannot yet be calculated from the sources reviewed. Global organic sales are not a valid proxy for Botswana revenue. The number of contracted turmeric farmers is a lead, not a full addressable market. A bottom-up estimate needs:
- Number of farms and producer groups by crop and district.
- Current and target certification standards and destination markets.
- Current certification, travel, inspection, recordkeeping and preparation costs.
- Number of operators willing and eligible to pay for local support.
- Buyers' actual requirements and contracted volumes.
- Group sizes and costs under permitted ICS models.
- Repeat/annual renewal rates and support costs.
- Expected non-payment, failed inspections, conversion-period and crop-failure risks.

Calculate at least three cases—conservative, base and upside—using verified interview and quote data. Separate service revenue from farmers' crop revenue and do not count a buyer's projected product value as certification-provider revenue.

## 7. Competitive and partner map to validate

1. **BOBS:** clarify applicable Botswana standards, label/claim rules, existing schemes and the Bureau's current role.
2. **Ministry of Lands and Agriculture:** confirm the organic turmeric programme, current farmer numbers, harvest/export outcomes, programme partners and any farmer support needs.
3. **Existing certifiers:** request current Botswana coverage, product categories, standard options, group-certification eligibility, inspection travel model, fee schedules and renewal costs. Begin with providers appearing in official registers, including Kiwa BCS as a lead from the EU listing, and compare other providers directly.
4. **SAOSO / PGS South Africa:** request advice on PGS design, cross-border applicability, governance, fees and lessons from established local assurance.
5. **IFOAM – Organics International / IFOAM Southern African Network:** explore PGS training and technical guidance; this is not a substitute for legal recognition.
6. **Producer groups, aggregators and buyers:** identify real procurement requirements before choosing a standard.
7. **Laboratories and technical specialists:** confirm test methods, accreditation scope, sample transport, turnaround times and costs where residue or soil/water testing is relevant. Testing alone does not certify organic production.

All partner status, fees and availability remain unverified until direct confirmation.

## 8. First 30-day validation sprint

### Days 1–5: Regulatory and market map
- Ask BOBS for written guidance on organic standards, labelling, certification, and any relevant national work.
- Obtain the latest EU control-body list and verify the exact Botswana/product scope directly with each candidate certifier.
- Ask the turmeric programme for current participant numbers, planted area, harvest results, actual shipments, farmer costs and the roles of certifiers and buyers.
- Confirm whether local PGS claims or logos can be used lawfully and how they must be described.

### Days 6–15: Buyer and farmer discovery
Conduct consent-based interviews with at least 10 producers across sizes/crops, 3 aggregators/exporters/processors, 3 buyers, 2 certification providers and relevant public/technical stakeholders. This is a proposed sample, not a statistically representative survey. Record anonymised evidence and distinguish observed facts from opinions.
Questions: What standard does the buyer require? What did the last certification cycle cost in total? Which costs are travel, audit, preparation, tests and administration? What caused delays or failed applications? Would group support help? Who pays? What would make the buyer accept the result?

### Days 16–22: Cost and service prototype
- Get written quotes from at least two relevant certifiers.
- Model individual vs group costs, including inspection, internal control, training, travel, administration, technology, annual renewal and corrective action.
- Create a paper-first, offline-capable recordkeeping prototype using synthetic farm data.
- Test it against one target standard with a qualified practitioner. Do not use real farm data without consent and safeguards.

### Days 23–30: Decision gate
Proceed to a small pilot only if there is:
- Written regulatory/standards clarity.
- A willing producer group and a buyer or credible route to market.
- A qualified certification partner for export-oriented products, where required.
- Evidence of a measurable cost, access or readiness problem.
- A viable cost model and impartiality safeguards.

Otherwise, pause or narrow the service to general agricultural traceability and standards-readiness support.

## 9. Proposed success measures

Track:
- Farms/groups assessed and enrolled (not merely contacted).
- Percentage with complete records and traceability.
- Inspection preparation hours per farm.
- Fully loaded cost per farm and per group, before and after support.
- First-pass inspection/compliance rate and corrective actions closed.
- Time from application to decision (reported separately from the certifier's own timeline).
- Buyer acceptance and repeat purchase/contract outcomes.
- Farmer retention and cost burden.
- Complaints, fraud findings, suspensions and data incidents.
- Revenue, gross margin, renewal rate and cost to serve.

Do not use “number certified” as the sole success metric. Integrity, farmer economics, buyer acceptance and continued compliance matter just as much.

## 10. Principal risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Confusing PGS with export certification | Explicit labels, legal review and buyer confirmation |
| No willingness to pay | Interview buyers and producers before product build; test paid pilots |
| Crop/finance failure despite certification | Keep agronomic, working-capital and buyer-contract risks visible |
| Conflict between consultant and certifier | Separate roles and decisions; independent oversight |
| Greenwashing or false claims | Claims register, public status checks and enforcement procedure |
| One buyer or project dominates | Validate alternative crops, buyers and channels |
| Digital tool becomes expensive custom software | Begin with templates and existing free/open tools; build only after repeated need |
| Certification scope changes | Recheck official lists and scope before each export programme |
| Farmer data exposure | Data minimisation, consent, role-based access and retention limits |

## Decision

**Conditional go for research and a low-cost readiness-service pilot; no-go for immediately establishing a new certification body.** The best potential advantage is local execution plus group economics and farmer support, anchored to existing recognised standards and independent certification decisions.
