### VET-BW-001 — Senna italica
The 2026 Batswana review identifies Senna italica as the most-cited plant in its inventory, with 10 citations. The review covers nine livestock-condition categories and reports that only about 52% of the 116 documented plants have empirical data covering biological effects, safety and phytochemicals. citeturn0search0

### VET-BW-002 — Terminalia sericea
The Batswana review records T. sericea among the three most-cited plants. citeturn0search0 BUAN records Tswana-goat feeding research involving T. sericea and Boscia albitrunca, and Botswana ethnoveterinary literature records its use in livestock. citeturn0search1turn0search8

A broader pharmacological review reports antimicrobial, antiparasitic, wound-healing and other activities but also notes toxicity questions and specifically says scientific evidence against common animal parasites remains lacking. citeturn0search3

**Important safety signal:** a Botswana Ngamiland study measured arsenic, chromium, lead and nickel in twelve traditionally used medicinal plants, including T. sericea. citeturn0search4

**Next evidence:** provenance and soil exposure → plant part → contaminant testing → target-animal safety → controlled veterinary efficacy.

### VET-BW-003 — Aloe spp. / Aloe ferox
A Botswana goat study in Lentsweletau recorded Aloe spp. as the most frequently reported plant, used for diarrhoea, cough and mange, and explicitly called for laboratory confirmation for safe use. citeturn0search5turn0search8

Broader goat-parasite literature identifies Aloe ferox among plants with high fidelity for parasite control, while a review of Aloe vera/Aloe ferox documents extensive phytochemical and toxicological literature. citeturn0search9turn0search14

**Next evidence:** resolve species identity in Botswana records, distinguish Aloe spp. from A. ferox, document preparation and dose, and separate external wound use from oral use.

### VET-BW-005 — Ngamiland knowledge system
Peer-reviewed work reports broad ethnoveterinary knowledge among livestock farmers and herders in Ngamiland, including treatment/prevention, assistance with births, fractures and range-management responses. The authors recommend further investigation and possible integration into veterinary extension, subject to evidence. citeturn0search10

## Commercial interpretation

These records are **RESEARCH-SUPPORTED SIGNALS**, not veterinary products.

Potential legitimate enterprise routes are:
- evidence/research dossiers;
- species authentication and sample coordination with qualified institutions;
- sustainable cultivation/supply research;
- laboratory testing coordination;
- agricultural extension information products;
- farmer-record and decision-support systems;
- sourcing/processing equipment after a real buyer requirement.

Do not market therapeutic claims from this evidence alone.

## Traditional knowledge controls

Before commercialisation, investigate applicable access, traditional-knowledge, biodiversity, benefit-sharing, collection and intellectual-property requirements. Record knowledge source and consent/provenance where research permits.
